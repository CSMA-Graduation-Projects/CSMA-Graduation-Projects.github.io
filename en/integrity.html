Academic integrity applies throughout topic selection, reading, implementation, experimentation and writing. A thorough pre-submission check is necessary but cannot replace accurate working records. This page distinguishes citation and similarity checks, checks for presentation and other non-academic errors, and disclosure of AI use, with steps for self-review and traceable corrections.

Check authenticity and provenance first

Every important thesis claim should be traceable to its supporting literature, data, run records or analysis. Distinguish results reported by the original authors, results you have actually reproduced and hypotheses still to be tested. When comparing with published results, identify their source and conditions. A successful demonstration, a completed run and sufficient support for a research conclusion represent different levels of evidence.

Experiment records should include at least the code version, environment, data version and split, configuration, execution command, time, exit status, logs and raw outputs. Retain failures, timeouts and anomalies, including or excluding them under rules explained in advance. Do not selectively retain favourable samples to improve a metric without reporting it. Generate figures and tables from saved data, retain processing scripts and check units, sample sizes, errors and captions.

For third-party code, models, images and data, check provenance, licences, use restrictions and citation requirements. Availability for download does not imply unrestricted permission to publish. For studies involving people, interviews, experiments or personal data, determine before recruitment or collection whether ethics review, informed consent or authorisation is required, and follow university procedures. A self-check completed after the research cannot stand in for prior approval.

Understand and address each citation and similarity issue

Keep citation records while writing: where a claim comes from, its location in the source and how it supports your argument. Identify direct quotations, and organise paraphrases in your own words with accurate attribution. Read and verify the references you cite; avoid nonexistent titles, authors, years or DOIs. Changing a few words does not make another person's ideas, figures or code your own work.

  1. Confirm rules and versions. Check the current official notice for the designated platform, material to be checked, submission contents, decision criteria and revision or retesting process. Do not invent an unconfirmed threshold.
  2. Check content and citations. Match in-text citations with bibliography entries, identify sources for figures and tables, and explain contributions and reuse in collaborative work or your own earlier outputs.
  3. Use the designated channel. Submit the complete draft reviewed by your supervisor and record its filename, version, date and checksum. Do not upload unpublished theses to unverified checking websites.
  4. Read the report item by item. Locate matching passages and assess citation accuracy, problematic wording and whether standard definitions need explanation. Correct the underlying issue and retain the reason and location of each change.
  5. Follow the required review procedure. Use formal channels for any retest or approval and retain reports and receipts. Passing a similarity check does not establish research quality, appropriate authorship or permitted data use.
Correct the work rather than trying to evade detection. Do not turn text into images, insert irrelevant text, substitute characters deliberately or fabricate citations to reduce a reported score. This page sets no universal similarity threshold and does not equate a score directly with a finding of misconduct.

Check presentation and other non-academic errors

Some Chinese universities use the term “cha-fei” for checks of non-academic errors, including typos, formatting, figures and tables, numbering, bibliographic details and layout. This is distinct from a similarity check. Confirm whether your university requires a formal check, its order, sampling procedure and assessment criteria in the local requirements record. The following methods can also be used for general writing-quality review.

Before official submission, reopen the exact file to be uploaded rather than inferring its version from the filename.

Explain, verify and take responsibility for AI use

First check the applicable rules of the university, course, competition and publication venue. Record the tool name and version, date, purpose, affected sections or code, and your verification method. Accurately distinguish language editing, code suggestions, information organisation and content generation; disclose use in the required place and format. An assistive tool cannot replace research and reasoning you are required to perform yourself.

Verify AI-provided references, facts, formulas, code and interpretations individually. Read and test code and check its licence; successful execution alone does not prove correctness. Do not present generated examples as real experiment records or user feedback. Enter unpublished papers, personal information, review comments or restricted data only when both the tool and the proposed use of those materials are authorised.

If the university requires AI-generated-content detection, record the policy version, interpretation of results and review route, then follow the applicable process. This page sets no AI-detection threshold, provides no evasion methods and does not automatically infer a violation from one score. If questions arise, retain writing versions, notes, experiments and revision history for review by authorised personnel.

Review anonymity beyond the main text

Read the specific anonymity requirements before creating the review copy from the approved thesis version. Areas commonly requiring attention include the cover, acknowledgements, author and supervisor details, research outputs, self-citation wording, project information, headers and footers, appendices, text inside images, attachments and filenames. Anonymity does not mean removing necessary scholarly references. Handle identifying information under the university's rules while maintaining academic accuracy.

Check PDF or document properties for authors and affiliations, comments, tracked changes and hidden content. Also inspect supplementary material, demonstration videos, repository links, READMEs, commit history and access pages for identifying information. If reviewers need code, use an approved anonymisation method; do not destroy repository history or delete original materials to achieve anonymity. Ask the department how to provide materials if reviewability and anonymity requirements appear to conflict.

Retain the complete thesis, the review copy and a record linking them, along with file checksums and system submission receipts. If an important issue is found after the version is frozen, contact the designated staff and follow the withdrawal or correction procedure. Do not privately replace the file and still claim it is the same reviewed version.

Make corrections traceable and reviewable

First locate the affected version and scope, then distinguish layout errors, insufficient evidence, inappropriate citation and potential integrity issues. Pause further submission of the affected material, preserve original records and consult your supervisor and the appropriate staff. For each comment, record your response, change location, evidence and unresolved matters. Explain why an unadopted suggestion was not followed; “revised” alone is not an adequate response.

Record group review, formal similarity and presentation checks, external review and defense outcomes separately. Suspected misconduct is determined and handled through university rules and formal procedures. For a disputed academic evaluation, consult the university's published review channels. Keep individual comments, detection reports and review materials access-controlled rather than displaying them on the public website.
