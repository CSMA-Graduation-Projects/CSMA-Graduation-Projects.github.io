## Bring a view to discuss
{: #prepare-a-view}

How much a supervision meeting can achieve depends partly on preparation. A student can begin with the question they most need to resolve, their current interpretation and the evidence behind it. When two experiments produce different results, for example, they might explain which factor they suspect and what information is still missing. An early interpretation need not be correct to be useful: it allows the supervisor to follow the student's reasoning and see where clarification would help.

Preparation does not require a long presentation. The relevant version, key results and approaches already tried can make a discussion more productive than a collection of screenshots from the whole week. If the work is incomplete, explain where it stopped and how that affects later tasks. A [progress report template](/en/templates.html#weekly) can help organise the material. Its purpose is to support an explanation the student is ready to discuss and question.

## Address the questions that affect the direction
{: #set-priorities}

A supervisor may notice problems with the research question, experimental design, writing and formatting in the same piece of work. Feedback can distinguish issues that affect the conclusion from details to improve in a later revision. If the methods being compared do not use equivalent conditions, it is useful to first examine whether the experiment answers the research question, then refine the figure and its wording. Making that order explicit helps students use their time well.

Advice should also reflect the student's experience. A beginner may need a demonstration and explanation; a more experienced student can first propose a revision. Supervisors can explain the reasoning behind a suggestion and check that its scope is understood. Students can respond by describing what they plan to change, what they hope to observe and which conditions remain uncertain. This brief exchange can reveal different interpretations of the same instruction before they shape several days of work.

## Leave room for unresolved disagreement
{: #record-disagreements}

Different interpretations are a normal part of research discussion. A student may think a result supports a conclusion while a supervisor believes another explanation needs to be ruled out. They can turn that disagreement into a question to investigate through further reading, a data check or an additional comparison. A meeting does not need to settle every question for useful work to continue, provided both people understand what evidence is needed next.

Students should explain the grounds for an objection and take time to understand the concern behind the feedback. Supervisors can distinguish requirements that must be followed, judgments supported by current evidence and preferences about how to work. This leaves room to discuss legitimate alternatives. If time or resources cannot resolve a disagreement, a narrower conclusion and an explicitly open question may be appropriate. Formal program requirements still need to be checked against the applicable institutional rules and procedures.

## Answer the advice with the next piece of work
{: #follow-through}

Before the meeting ends, agree briefly on what to do first, where the result will be available, when to discuss it again and what support is needed. The student can put this in a [meeting and action record](/en/templates.html#meeting), with the supervisor confirming key decisions and priorities. A short record is enough if it lets someone returning to the task a few days later understand why that choice was made.

The next meeting can begin with the previous agreement: what changed, what remains incomplete and what has been learned. Supervisors can then adjust their support without having to redesign the entire task each time. As students become more able to recognise problems, compare options and explain their choices, discussion can move towards deeper research judgments. The [day-to-day collaboration guide](/en/management.html#weekly-and-meeting) offers practical arrangements; meeting frequency and feedback methods can be agreed again as the work changes.
